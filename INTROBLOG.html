Pro-Labour Games?

Behind SpekWork and Escape the Gig Economy

January 2019

In choosing a name you necessarily search for a certain stickiness. Spec work-- unpaid work undertaken in the hope of perhaps being paid -- offers a caustic sort of stick, adhering, as it does, to our creative communities. Spec work demands labour in the form of examples, proofs, or the plain exploitation of creative workers, in advance of, and often without, fair remuneration. This is the residue of power imbalance that characterizes the so-called “gig economy,” and clings to the conditions of cultural production in what has been termed platform capitalism. Our studio combines a cast of culture workers:  urban lefties toting Macbooks, liberal arts degrees and the crushing debt that affords these. We’ve worked in education, music, the fine and performing arts, and we’ve each of us done a great deal of freelancing.

Ours is arguably the first generation forced to confront the lie of post-war social progress, and this bears out psychological and political consequences. Before our jobs in digital media were even possible, the last century saw hardwon job securities eroded by a process of state- and corporation-led attrition. The result is a pervasively precarious workforce no longer meaningfully protected by unions nor, increasingly, by the state. Working multiple gigs to make the minimum payments on student loans and credit cards has left many millennials without the money that would traditionally afford a meaningful sense of future security. Instead, many opt for the relative techno-utopia of social media-based approval and video game victory. The dopamine rush these platforms offer, function in lieu of workplace success and social status traditionally provided by the disappearing middle class and its cults of nuclear family-making and home owning. As the Boomers self-medicated the trauma of imperial dirty wars, and the deracination of globalization and suburban sprawl with booze and TV, millennials increasingly turn to personal devices as their addictive coping mechanism of choice [1].

The rise of the gig economy -- powered by the modern faith that emerging technologies enable people to work more freely -- has resulted in billions of dollars form company executives, but it has also exposed the requisite host of exploited contract workers embedded in the code. Uber’s driver app is gamified to encourage longer and harder working hours, throwing employees into reckless competition.[2] It’s no different if your trade is driving or programming, labour exploitation occurs across the labour ecology. Despite the putative notion of socially responsible silicon valley startups, employee wellbeing is far from the standard in such tech-driven industries.[4] For instance, Rockstar Games received backlash for its exploitative employer practices.[3] Furthery, the global labour action taken by Amazon workers this passed summer on the company’s Prime Day, and recent reports of other logistics company’s labour conditions approaching those of early industrialization, signal the urgent need for organized labour and political solidarity in the fresh wake of human wreckage cut by new technologies.

The responsibility of ‘play’ within this emergent context of labour unrest was underscored by streamers Amazon-owned Twitch striking in solidarity with factory workers. This critical connection is not so indirect as one might suppose at first blush. Play and entertainment are used to normalize politics through culture. The US military quickly adopted the popular first person shooter genre as a method of recruitment and to normalize its imperial global presence.  [5] Play is something historically used to adapt new social and technological norms. As Mary Flanagan notes in Critical Play:

Shifts in play have historically mirrored shifts in technologies. This is evident in the invention of organized doll play and “playing house” during the U.S. industrial revolution, when gender roles needed to be reasserted due to changing labor [sic] conditions. The link among the reorganization of banking, financial systems, and property ownership in twentieth-century U.S. culture mirrored the rise of financially focused board games such as Monopoly.[6]

As our social and leisure time is increasingly mediated through proprietary technologies, we adapt ourselves to these standards. The techniques that driver Uber workers harder and keep you scrolling on Facebook longer are strategies of game design. Gaming is changing the way we work.  We work when we play, with monetized tracking algorithms extracting our digital labour, and play through our work with the appification of services like Uber or Fiverr. These jobs already existed, but slick branding and a decentralized labour force enables a wider audience to prey on increasingly precarious workers. We need to be having conversations about work, and the state of labour politics today, perhaps especially because it feels old-fashioned. While cultural ideas of work haven’t changed as fast as our technology, politicians and elites actively undermine good work and the process of collective bargaining that protects good jobs.

SpekWork’s first release, Gigco: Escape the Gig Economy explores this changing world of work and play. Post-holiday discount season is upon us, and while you shop, many gig workers (and machines) are working hard overtime, to stock, ship, and deliver gifts.In Gigco, you play a worker moving boxes to point A to point B. Your nemesis is the automated shipping robot, who’s ready to take your job at the given chance. If you can’t maneuver around the robots and complete your shift, the lights dim; the warehouse transitions to full post-worker automation. The Gigco company’s social media connects the protagonist to the greater universe, and the sprawling narrative of global workers’ confrontation with automation emerges. The game is being released exclusively on the Apple app store on January 21, 2019, a date popularized as “Blue Monday.” The third Monday of the year is claimed the most depressing, when holidays debtors return to work, bound by our credit card overlords to 49 more work weeks. Blue Monday was conceptualized in 2005, and fourteen years and one international banking crisis later we’ve only increased our demand for cheap money, labour, and entertainment - at great expense to our people.

Class consciousness is anything but spontaneous. It grew when there were mediating agencies that propagated it: political parties, trade unions, working class associations. Neoliberalism destroyed or subdued those agencies. After years of defeat there is a collective depression on our side.

Mark Fisher
